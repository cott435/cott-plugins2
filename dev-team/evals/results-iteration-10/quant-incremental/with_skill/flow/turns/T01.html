144	## Phase 2: Evaluate & converge
145	
146	1. **Cluster** what resonated into 2-3 meaningfully different directions.
147	2. **Stress-test** each: user value (painkiller or vitamin?), feasibility (cost, hardest part), differentiation (would someone switch?), and for ML and data projects, data: does usable data exist, can we use it, what's the non-ML baseline? 150	# Refinement & Evaluation Criteria
151	
152	Use this rubric during Phase 2 (Evaluate & Converge) to stress-test idea directions. Not every criterion applies to every idea — use judgment about which dimensions matter most for the specific context.
153	
154	## Core Evaluation Dimensions
155	
156	### 1. User Value
157	
158	The most important dimension. If the value isn't clear, nothing else matters.
159	
160	**Painkiller vs. Vitamin:**
161	- **Painkiller:** Solves an acute, frequent problem. Users will actively seek this out. They'll switch from their current solution. Signs: people describe the problem with emotion, they've built workarounds, they'll pay for a solution.
162	- **Vitamin:** Nice to have. Makes something marginally better. Users won't go out of their way. Signs: people nod politely, say "that's cool," then don't change behavior.
163	
164	**Questions to ask:**
165	- Can you name 3 specific people who have this problem right now?
166	- What are they doing today instead? (The real competitor is always the current workaround.)
167	- Would they switch from their current approach? What would make them switch?
168	- How often do they encounter this problem? (Daily problems > monthly problems)
169	- Is this a "pull" problem (users are asking for this) or a "push" problem (you think they should want this)?
170	
171	**Red flags:**
172	- "Everyone could use this" — if you can't name a specific user, the value isn't clear
173	- "It's like X but better" — marginal improvements rarely drive adoption
174	- The problem is real but rare — high intensity but low frequency rarely justifies a product
175	
176	### 2. Feasibility
177	
178	Can you actually build this? Not just technically, but practically.
179	
180	**Technical feasibility:**
181	- Does the core technology exist and work reliably?
182	- What's the hardest technical problem? Is it a known-hard problem or a novel one?
183	- Are there dependencies on third parties, APIs, or data sources you don't control?
184	- What's the minimum technical stack needed? (If the answer is "a lot," that's a signal.)
185	
186	**Resource feasibility:**
187	- What's the minimum team/effort to build an MVP?
188	- Does it require specialized expertise you don't have?
189	- Are there regulatory, legal, or compliance requirements?
190	
191	**Time-to-value:**
192	- How quickly can you get something in front of users?
193	- Is there a version that delivers value in days/weeks, not months?
194	- What's the critical path? What has to happen first?
195	
196	**Red flags:**
197	- "We just need to solve [very hard research problem] first"
198	- Multiple dependencies that all need to work simultaneously
199	- MVP still requires months of work — likely not minimal enough
200	
201	### 3. Differentiation
202	
203	What makes this genuinely different? Not better — *different*.
204	
205	**Questions to ask:**
206	- If a user described this to a friend, what would they say? Is that description compelling?
207	- What's the one thing this does that nothing else does? (If you can't name one, that's a problem.)
208	- Is this differentiation durable? Can a competitor copy it in a week?
209	- Is the difference something users actually care about, or just something builders find interesting?
210	
211	**Types of differentiation (strongest to weakest):**
212	1. **New capability:** Does something that was previously impossible
213	2. **10x improvement:** So much better on a key dimension that it changes behavior
214	3. **New audience:** Brings an existing capability to people who were excluded
215	4. **New context:** Works in a situation where existing solutions fail
216	5. **Better UX:** Same capability, dramatically simpler experience
217	6. **Cheaper:** Same thing, lower cost (weakest — easily competed away)
218	
219	**Red flags:**
220	- Differentiation is entirely about technology, not user experience
221	- "We're faster/cheaper/prettier" without a structural reason why
222	- The feature that differentiates is not the feature users care most about
223	
224	**Internal tools, research code, and personal projects:** "would someone switch?" becomes "is this better than the notebook/spreadsheet/script we use today?" For forecasting work, differentiation is the accuracy gain itself — and the question is whether it survives held-out data and inputs known at forecast time, not whether it's novel.
225	
226	### 4. Data & ML Feasibility
227	
228	Apply this when the idea depends on data or a learned model. For these projects it is often the dimension that decides everything — a great idea with no usable data is not feasible.
229	
230	**Do we need ML at all?**
231	- What's the simplest non-ML baseline (a rule, a threshold, a heuristic, a linear model)? How good is it?
232	- How much better than the baseline does the model need to be to matter? If nobody can say, the value isn't clear.
233	- What does a wrong prediction cost, and who catches it?
234	
235	**Does the data exist?**
236	- Where would the data come from — data we own, a public dataset, a paid API? (Name candidates to test feasibility, checked from their public docs and pricing pages only; the final choice is made in the architecture. A risk that could kill the idea and that the docs can't settle goes under Open questions, for the architecture to probe.)
237	- Is there enough history and coverage (time span, entities, classes, regions)?
238	- For supervised learning: are there labels? Who made them, and would two people agree?
239	- Can we legally use it for this purpose? Licensing, terms of service, personal or patient data?
240	- What does it cost, now and at the scale we'd need?
241	
242	**Is the data trustworthy for this use?**
243	- **Leakage / look-ahead:** would the model or its evaluation see anything that wasn't known at decision time?
244	- **Selection bias:** does the data quietly drop the failures (decommissioned meters, churned users, failed runs)?
245	- **Drift:** is the past a good guide to the future here? How fast does it go stale?
246	- **Point-in-time:** for time series especially, can we reconstruct what was known *at each time*, including later corrections?
247	
248	**Can we tell if it works?**
249	- What metric, on what held-out data, compared to what baseline?
250	- Can it be tested before it's trusted — offline evaluation, replay on history, shadow mode, human review?
251	
252	**Red flags:**
253	- "We'll figure out the data later" — for ML, the data *is* the project
254	- No baseline, so any result looks like success
255	- Labels that nobody has checked for consistency
256	- Offline results that look too good (usually leakage, look-ahead, or unrealistic costs)
257	- Data you can't legally use for this purpose, or that costs more than the project's budget
258	
259	## Assumption Audit
260	
261	For every idea direction, explicitly list assumptions in three categories:
262	
263	### Must Be True (Dealbreakers)
264	Assumptions that, if wrong, kill the idea entirely. These need validation before building.
265	
266	Example: "Users will share their data with us" — if they won't, the entire product doesn't work.
267	
268	### Should Be True (Important)
269	Assumptions that significantly impact success but don't kill the idea. You can adjust the approach if these are wrong.
270	
271	Example: "Users prefer self-serve over talking to a person" — if wrong, you need a different go-to-market, but the core product can still work.
272	
273	### Might Be True (Nice to Have)
274	Assumptions about secondary features or optimizations. Don't validate these until the core is proven.
275	
276	Example: "Users will want to share their results with teammates" — a growth feature, not a core value proposition.
277	
278	## Decision Framework
279	
280	When choosing between directions, rank on this matrix:
281	
282	|                    | High Feasibility | Low Feasibility |
283	|--------------------|-------------------|-----------------|
284	| **High Value**     | Do this first     | Worth the risk   |
285	| **Low Value**      | Only if trivial   | Don't do this    |
286	
287	Then use differentiation as the tiebreaker between options in the same quadrant.
288	
289	## MVP Scoping Principles
290	
291	When defining MVP scope for the chosen direction:
292	
293	1. **One job, done well.** The MVP should nail exactly one user job. Not three jobs done partially.
294	2. **The riskiest assumption first.** The MVP's primary purpose is to test the assumption most likely to be wrong.
295	3. **Time-box, not feature-list.** "What can we build and test in [timeframe]?" is better than "What features do we need?"
296	4. **The 'Not Doing' list is mandatory.** Explicitly name what you're cutting and why. This prevents scope creep and forces honest prioritization.
297	5. **If it's not embarrassing, you waited too long.** The first version should feel incomplete to the builder. If it doesn't, you over-built.
