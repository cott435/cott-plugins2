# Ideation: understand, expand, converge

Phases 1 and 2 of every run, New and Extend alike. The mode file says what to read before Phase 1 and what Phase 2 must also settle.

## Phase 1: Understand & expand

1. **Restate the idea** as a "How Might We" problem statement.
2. **Ask 3-5 sharpening questions**, no more, with `AskUserQuestion`: who it's for, what success looks like, real constraints, what's been tried, why now. Don't proceed until you know who it's for and what success looks like.
3. **Generate 5-8 variations**, each with a one-line reason it exists. Lenses: inversion, constraint removal, audience shift, combination, simplification, 10x version, expert lens. `frameworks.md` has more; pick what fits.

In a codebase, ground variations in what exists and cite files. Read `docs/references.md` too; an idea cites a reference as prior art by `RF-NN`, never its path.

## Phase 2: Evaluate & converge

1. **Cluster** what resonated into 2-3 meaningfully different directions.
2. **Stress-test** each: user value (painkiller or vitamin?), feasibility (cost, hardest part), differentiation (would someone switch?), and for ML and data projects, data: does usable data exist, can we use it, what's the non-ML baseline? "No usable data" is a feasibility failure, not an open question. Rubric: `refinement-criteria.md`.
3. **Surface hidden assumptions:** what you're betting is true, what could kill it, what you're ignoring for now.
4. **Get a decision** on a direction before writing anything.
# Roadmap stages

Here's where we start — no brief exists yet, so this is a fresh project. Before anything gets written down, I want to sharpen the idea.

## The idea, restated

**How might we** build a small set of 2D grid environments, each teaching one reinforcement-learning concept, so that building them and training agents on them is how *you* learn RL — with Snake + DQN (Deep Q-Network: a neural net that estimates how good each action is) as the first lesson?

## One honest pushback first

Snake is a classic, but it's a **harder first DQN problem than it looks**. Rewards are sparse early on: a random snake rarely finds food. Late-game play needs long-horizon planning, because the snake can trap itself in its own body. And the way you show the board to the agent (a few hand-picked features vs. the raw grid) changes the outcome more than any DQN tweak. That's actually a great lesson, but it means "my agent isn't learning" could be a bug, bad hyperparameters or a bad observation, and a first-time RL learner can't tell those apart yet. Either way, we should plan a **non-learning baseline** (random play, plus a greedy "move toward food" rule) and a **tiny-grid sanity check** so you can tell broken from just slow.

## Sharpening questions

1. **Your starting point:** how much ML/deep learning have you done? Have you trained a neural net in PyTorch (or similar) before? And how much RL theory do you know: Q-learning, Bellman equations, none yet?
2. **Build from scratch or use libraries?** Do you want to write DQN yourself (replay buffer, target network, epsilon-greedy) to learn it, or use a library like Stable-Baselines3 and focus on the environment? I'd lean toward from scratch, since learning is the point, but it's your call.
3. **What does "done" look like for Env 1?** For example: "the agent averages 20+ food per game on a 10×10 grid, I can watch it play, and I can explain why it works." Or is it more about understanding than a score?
4. **Hardware and time:** laptop CPU only, or do you have a GPU? And is this a weekend project or a few weeks of evenings?
5. **The "lab" part:** for later environments, do you want a shared structure (a common environment interface, training runner and plots, so Env 2 plugs right in), or is each environment its own standalone experiment for now?

## Variations to react to

1. **Straight Snake + hand-rolled DQN.** The plan as stated. A classic, with lots of reference material to compare against.
2. **Curriculum Snake** (simplify): start on a 5×5 grid where the snake doesn't grow, then turn on growth, then scale up. Each step isolates one difficulty: finding food, then avoiding yourself, then long-horizon planning.
3. **Tabular Q-learning first, then DQN** (expert lens): solve a tiny Snake with a plain lookup table, then swap in a neural net and watch what changes. You learn *why* DQN exists, not just how.
4. **Observation shoot-out** (combination): the same DQN on three views of the board (11 hand-crafted features, a local window, the full grid with a small conv net). Shows that representation matters in RL.
5. **"Break it on purpose" lab** (inversion): remove the target network, the replay buffer or reward shaping one at a time, and plot what fails. Ablations make the abstract parts of DQN concrete.
6. **Gymnasium-compatible lab** (10x version): every environment follows the standard Gymnasium interface, so later you can test your own agents against library ones, and theirs against yours.
7. **Visual-first:** a live viewer and learning curves are built in from day one, because watching the agent improve is half the motivation.

My early instinct is that **1 + 2 + 3 + 5** fit together as one direction: tabular on a tiny grid → DQN → ablations, plus a baseline throughout. But your answers to questions 1, 2 and 5 will decide it.

Answer whichever questions you like and tell me which variations grab you. Then I'll narrow to 2–3 directions and stress-test them before writing the brief.
