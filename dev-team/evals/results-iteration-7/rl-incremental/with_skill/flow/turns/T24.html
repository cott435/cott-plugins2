# Idea: Predator-prey foraging (co-evolution)

**Status:** Draft · **Last updated:** 2026-10-17 · **Brief:** [../brief.md](../brief.md)

## In one sentence
This adds a third environment, the fish tank with food pellets, three prey and a learning predator, so that I can learn co-evolution and self-play by watching each side's learning change what the other has to learn.

## The problem
Snake and the tank each have one learner facing a fixed world. Real multi-agent problems have learners facing other learners, so the target keeps moving: a trick that works today stops working once the opponent adapts. That non-stationarity, the cycling it causes and the standard fixes are the next concept, and like the others it only sticks by building and watching it.

## Who it's for
Me, the brief's learner, once PPO works in the tank.

## How it fits
- **Serves:** Goal 1 (explain why it works), Goal 2 (honest baselines), Goal 3 (see learning happen), Goal 4 (reuse the shared core)
- **Touches:** the tank's geometry, whisker rays, continuous swimming and catch rule (TANK-02–TANK-10); PPO (PG-02–PG-08); the flee fish (BASE-05) and the T4 chasing logic (TANK-13) as scripted opponents; comparison and plots (EVAL-02–EVAL-04). The core stays as approved (CORE-01–CORE-08): both sides have the same body.
- **Brief changes:** None.
- **Stage:** New S3 on acceptance.

## Recommended direction
**The world.** The S2 tank with about 10 food pellets that respawn at random spots when eaten, 3 prey fish and 1 predator fish. Every fish has the same body: the tank's whisker rays (now also seeing pellets and telling prey from predator) and continuous turn and throttle. The predator is faster and catches anything it touches; caught prey are out. An episode ends when all prey are caught or at a time limit. Each row of the environment carries a team label, and a small two-team agent routes prey rows to one PPO policy and the predator's row to another, so the shared core, training loop and viewer need no change.

**Rewards, not strictly zero-sum.** Prey earn +1 per pellet and −1 when caught. The predator earns +1 per catch and pays a small cost every step, so it can't win by waiting.

**A curriculum in four steps:**
1. Each side learns against a scripted opponent first: prey against the T4-style chaser, the predator against flee prey. These are the single-learner baselines.
2. Naive simultaneous self-play: both sides update every rollout. Expect instability, on purpose.
3. Alternating freezes: train one side against the other's frozen policy, then swap.
4. An opponent pool: each side trains against randomly drawn past snapshots of the other, the standard fix for cycling.

**Measuring a moving target.** Learning curves alone mislead when both sides move. Every run saves periodic snapshots of both sides. Two measures run throughout: each snapshot scored against the fixed scripted references, and a cross-play matrix of every prey snapshot against every predator snapshot, drawn as a heatmap. The heatmap is where the arms race, and any cycling, becomes visible.

**Stretch.** A sweep of the predator's speed, to find where the contest stays balanced.

## Data
None external. Evaluation reuses the held-out seeds, 5 training seeds and the `null-hypothesis-testing` skill. Prey are scored on pellets eaten and time survived; the predator on catches. The cross-play matrix and snapshot scores are plotted following the `dataviz` skill.

## Alternatives considered
- A new environment from scratch — time spent on physics, not co-evolution.
- Different bodies per side — needs per-team spaces in the core, a revision just before M1 builds it.
- A strictly zero-sum reward — simpler maths, but a waiting predator and an idle prey become stable answers.
- Only learning curves as evidence — can't separate "prey got better" from "predator got worse".

## What success looks like
- The learned predator beats the scripted chaser against flee prey; the learned prey beat the flee fish against the scripted chaser (5 seeds, significant).
- The cross-play heatmap shows the arms race over training.
- Training against the opponent pool measurably reduces cycling compared with naive self-play.
- I can watch predator and prey in the same viewer as Snake and the tank.

## What we're betting on
- PPO from S2 learns both roles within the hour-per-environment budget.
- Cycling shows up clearly enough at this scale to see it, and to see the pool reduce it.
- Same-body fish give each side enough room to develop different strategies.

## Not doing
- Populations of several learning predators, or prey that communicate or share a team reward — each is its own concept.
- Different bodies or senses per side — keeps the core unchanged.
- League-style training (exploiters, ratings) beyond the snapshot pool — a later concept if this one lands.

## Open questions
- Sizes: predator speed and catch radius, pellet count and respawn rule, time limit, per-step predator cost, snapshot interval and pool size.
- How "reduces cycling" is measured: a number from the cross-play matrix, compared across training schemes.
- Whether the speed sweep is a requirement or only a reported stretch.
